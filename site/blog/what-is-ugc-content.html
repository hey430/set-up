UGC Ads

What Is UGC? Why Creator-Led Ads Outsell Polished Brand Content

  • Sep 15, 2026
  • 3 min read
  • Haul House Team

UGC, short for user-generated content, is video made by real creators in a native, first-person style. Think unboxings, "get ready with me" try-ons, honest reviews and the classic haul. In advertising, "UGC ads" means paying creators to make that kind of content about your product, then running it organically or as paid media on TikTok, Meta and Instagram.

At Haul House, UGC is most of what we make. This guide covers what UGC is, why it keeps beating polished studio creative in short-form feeds, and what separates UGC that sells from UGC that just exists.

What counts as UGC?

The term started out meaning content that customers posted on their own. Today brands mostly use it to describe a style rather than a source. A UGC video usually has:

  • A person on camera, talking directly to the viewer, usually filmed on a phone.
  • A real setting, such as a kitchen, bathroom, car or desk, instead of a studio set.
  • Native editing: jump cuts, on-screen text, trending audio and platform-native pacing.
  • A recommendation framing, like "I tried this so you don't have to" instead of "Introducing the all-new…".

Common formats include hauls, unboxings, before-and-afters, "3 reasons I switched", tutorials, reaction videos and side-by-side comparisons.

Why UGC converts in short-form feeds

It looks like the feed, so it gets watched

People open TikTok and Reels to watch people, not ads. A video that looks and sounds like everything else in the feed doesn't trigger the "skip, this is an ad" reflex. That buys the extra seconds a product needs to make its case.

It borrows trust

A recommendation from a person the viewer relates to carries more weight than a claim from the brand itself. Good UGC creators show the product in use, react honestly and answer the objections a shopper actually has, like "Does it really…?", "Is it worth the price?" and "What's the catch?"

It's built to iterate

Studio shoots produce a few expensive assets. UGC produces many lighter ones, so you can test different hooks, creators and angles, then put spend behind what the data rewards. On TikTok Shop, where each affiliate video is its own storefront, that volume matters even more.

The goal isn't "content that looks amateur". It's content that looks native, with the strategy of a direct-response ad underneath.

What separates UGC that sells

Most weak UGC fails for the same few reasons: a slow opening, a vague benefit, or no reason to buy now. When we script UGC we build every video around four beats:

  1. The hook (0–3s). A visual or line that stops the thumb. We cover this in depth in our guide to scroll-stopping hooks.
  2. The problem or desire. Make the viewer feel the "before".
  3. The proof. Show the product doing the thing. Demonstration beats description.
  4. The call to action. Tell them exactly what to do: tap the orange cart, use the link, grab it before the promo ends.

How to brief UGC creators

A creator can only make what the brief makes possible. A strong UGC brief includes:

  • One core message: the single thing the viewer should remember.
  • Two or three hook options, so creators can pick the one that fits their voice.
  • Must-show moments, such as the texture, the before/after or the unboxing reveal.
  • Claims guardrails: what can and can't be said, especially for food, beauty and wellness.
  • Platform specs: length, aspect ratio, captions and whether the video will run as a Spark Ad or on Meta.
  • Room to be themselves. Over-scripting kills the authenticity you're paying for.

Where to run UGC

The same creator footage can power several channels, but each needs its own edit:

  • TikTok and TikTok Shop: sound-on, fast, trend-aware, with a shoppable product link.
  • Meta (Facebook and Instagram ads): stronger captions for sound-off viewing, and a clearer offer and CTA.
  • Instagram Reels: a little more polished and aspirational, while still person-led.

The short version: UGC works because it matches how people actually watch short-form video. The brands that win with it treat it as a system of hooks, creators, testing and iteration, not a one-off batch of videos.
